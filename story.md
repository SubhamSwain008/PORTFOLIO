# THE HOLLOW BETWEEN US
### *A story told in five wounds.*

---

## FOREWORD — WHAT THIS STORY IS

This is not a story about a man who loses his wife.
This is a story about a man who loses the *lie* he built where his wife used to be.

The player will think they are playing a survival game about a husband trying to save his wife from a cursed world beyond the fence. They are wrong. They are playing the last forty-seven seconds of a dying man's brain, stretched across hours, looping back on itself, hallucinating a rescue that already failed.

Everything in this game is a symptom.
The supplies. The fence. The stranger. The cave. The boss. Every single mechanic is Raju's mind lying to him so that he does not have to face what happened in the hall.

The game has four realms — **Hall Interior, Night World, Day World, Cave** — and each realm is a different stage of the same grief. The hall is the truth he is running from. The night world is his guilt, dressed up as things that hunt him. The day world is his denial, dressed up as sunlight. The cave is his subconscious, where what is buried finally has to be named. He cycles between them until he cannot lie to himself any longer.

This document is the authoritative bible of that truth. Build everything backwards from the ending.

---

## THE CAST

- **Raju** — The player. Thirty-four. Accountant. Soft hands. A man who has never been told "no" in a way that hurt him. A man who believes he is a good person because he has not *yet* been proven otherwise.
- **Meera** — His wife. Twenty-nine. Schoolteacher. Has loved him so completely and so quietly that he mistook her love for furniture. She is the axis the entire world spins on, and she is the one character Raju never truly sees until she is already gone.
- **Kaveri** — The other woman. She is not the villain. She is barely a person in this story. She is a mirror Raju looked into because he was tired of his own reflection.
- **The Stranger** — Tall, thin, faceless under a grey hood. Stands at fences. Speaks in the cadence of someone who has loved Raju for a very long time. He is not who he appears to be. He never was. *(See the full design document in the section **THE STRANGER — A CHARACTER STUDY** below.)*
- **The Thing in the Cave** — The boss. Wears Meera's wedding sari. Does not have Meera's face. Has something worse.
- **The Child That Was Never Named** — Twelve weeks along. Did not survive the fall. Threads through the entire game as whispers, as small handprints, as a crib that appears in rooms it should not be in.

---

## THE FOUR REALMS — HOW THE STORY MAPS TO THE GAME

The game has four playable zones: **Hall Interior, Night World, Day World, Cave.** Every symbolic beat of the story is assigned to one of these zones, so nothing in the narrative exists outside the map you are actually building. The realms are not arbitrary levels — they are **four stages of the same grief,** cycled through until Raju cannot lie to himself any longer.

### THE LOOP

```
   ┌───────────────────────────────┐
   │      HALL INTERIOR            │ ← the truth. the crime scene. the hub.
   │      (where he is bleeding)   │
   └─────────────┬─────────────────┘
                 │ step through the front door
                 ▼
   ┌───────────────────────────────┐
   │      NIGHT WORLD              │ ← his guilt, made monstrous.
   │      (cursed, hunted, dead)   │   the bestiary lives here.
   └─────────────┬─────────────────┘
                 │ step into the portal
                 ▼
   ┌───────────────────────────────┐
   │      DAY WORLD                │ ← his denial. a sunlit lie.
   │      (false memory realm)     │   corrupts with every visit.
   └─────────────┬─────────────────┘
                 │ return through the portal
                 │ enough supplies gathered
                 ▼
   ┌───────────────────────────────┐
   │      CAVE                     │ ← his subconscious.
   │      (the descent, 7 chambers)│   confession, not combat.
   └─────────────┬─────────────────┘
                 │ climb back out
                 ▼
   ┌───────────────────────────────┐
   │      HALL INTERIOR (warped)   │ ← the boss arena.
   │      → HALL INTERIOR (real)   │ ← the waking, the crawl.
   └───────────────────────────────┘
```

### 1. HALL INTERIOR — *"the truth"*

The physical home. The crime scene. The hub.

Narratively: **this is the only place where what is actually happening is real.** Raju and Meera are on the floor, bleeding, the entire game. Every other realm is a story his mind is telling to avoid this room. The player returns here between every outward expedition. Each return, something in the hall is *slightly more wrong than it was before* — a bloodstain moves, the brass lamp is on a different table, the crib reassembles one more piece, Meera's body is sometimes glimpsed on the floor for a single frame before the player's eye lands on it and it is no longer there.

The hall's job is to **leak the truth in increments** while the other realms try to drown it out. By the end, the hall has become un-ignorable.

- **Mechanical role:** hub. Save point. Inventory management. Between-run recovery.
- **Tone:** domestic horror. Silence. The ticking of a clock that has been broken for years.
- **Enemies:** none. The hall is safe. That is what makes it unbearable.
- **Truth revealed here:** everything, eventually, if the player pays attention.

### 2. NIGHT WORLD — *"his guilt, made monstrous"*

The moment Raju opens the front door in Act II, he steps into the night world. This is the dead, drained, hunted version of his neighborhood, wrapped in a sky the color of an old bruise. **Everything in the bestiary lives here.** Every monster is a piece of Raju dressed up as a thing that wants to kill him, because guilt only ever speaks to the guilty in the grammar of being hunted.

Assign each existing bestiary creature a symbolic role. The player should not be told this. They should feel it when they realize that every creature has a *reason* to be where it is.

- **Hellhound** = his **appetite.** The thing that drove him, hungry and stupid, to Kaveri's hotel room. Four-legged, massive, tireless. Hunts in straight lines. Always smells him first.
- **Shadow Spider** = his **lies.** The many small cover stories he told Meera for eighteen months. Eight legs, because lies need legs. Venomous bite — she was slow-poisoned by years of almost-truths. Hides in corners of rooms Meera kept clean.
- **Banshee Ghost** = **Meera's grief,** finally audible. For three years she held it silent. In the night world it has escaped her and floats without a body, screaming the thing she never said out loud. Cannot be killed. Can only be outrun.
- **Shadow Wraith** = **Raju's own death.** The glass shard in his belly, incarnate. Drags chains. Carries a scythe. Moves slowly because it does not need to hurry — it already lives inside him. *The wraith is the reason the flashlight freezes it: Raju cannot die while he is still illuminated, still narrating, still hallucinating the rescue. The moment he lowers the flashlight, the wraith catches up.*
- **Skull Specter** = **the unborn child's anger.** Small, fast, on fire. The one creature that should not be here — a baby should not burn. And yet.

When the player eventually understands this (via item descriptions, ambient dialogue, the cave's revelations), they cannot un-understand it. The bestiary is not a catalogue of monsters. It is **a family photograph of the things Raju did.**

- **Mechanical role:** primary survival loop. Supply gathering, combat, exploration, the Stranger at the fence.
- **Tone:** quiet, dead, hunted. The fear of being *known* by something that should not know you.
- **Enemies:** the full bestiary.
- **Truth revealed here:** that he is being hunted *on purpose,* and the hunters have reasons.

### 3. DAY WORLD — *"his denial"*

Accessed via the portal inside the night world — the same portal you already have wired in (`/realm?portal=true`).

The day world is his neighborhood **as it was,** or as he wants to remember it. Warm light. Birds singing (the *one* black bird from the night world is gone here). The sun actually moves across the sky. Meera's laundry is on the line. A neighbor waves. It is the world-as-it-should-have-been.

**It is the most terrifying realm in the game,** because it is the one that feels safe.

The day world is a lie. It corrupts a little with every visit. The player should not notice the corruption on visit one. By visit five, they should be afraid to come back here.

**Corruption rules (cumulative across visits):**
- **Visit 1:** Perfect. Warm. Meera is not home, but her tea is still steaming on the counter. A note says she has gone to the market and will be back soon. *(She never comes back. She has been gone for longer than Raju realizes.)*
- **Visit 2:** Same, but the photographs on the walls have Raju's face subtly removed from them. Not scratched out — just absent, like he was never photographed, like Meera was always standing next to empty air.
- **Visit 3:** The neighbor who waved on Visit 1 is still waving. He has not moved. He will wave forever. His mouth is the wrong shape for a smile.
- **Visit 4:** Raju can hear Meera humming in another room. He walks toward the humming. The room is always the next room over. He cannot catch her. The humming is the three-note motif.
- **Visit 5:** The day world's sky has a crack in it, top left. The sun behind the crack is a stadium light. If Raju looks directly at it, the sun blinks.
- **Visit 6+:** Blood starts appearing in small places. A single drop on the kitchen floor. A red fingerprint on the screen door. He will not see these unless he goes looking. They are the hall leaking through.
- **Final visit (just before the descent into the cave):** The day world is empty. The laundry line is bare. The tea on the counter has gone cold. The humming has stopped. A single yellow baby sock is on the kitchen floor. When Raju picks it up, the day world ends, and he is back in the night.

**Supplies in the day world are different from supplies in the night world.** The night world gives Raju *objects* — things from his marriage. The day world gives Raju **memories** — flavor-text-only items (*"the way she laughed when you burned the rice"*; *"the silence after you said nothing on her birthday last year"*). These are required to open certain doors in the cave, later. He cannot complete his descent without having stared, repeatedly, at the sunlit lie of what his marriage *wasn't.*

- **Mechanical role:** secondary resource loop, memory-collection, breather space that gets less breathable every trip.
- **Tone:** warm, nostalgic, *wrong.* Like a childhood home that smells like someone else's house now.
- **Enemies:** none — until the corruption thresholds are crossed, at which point a single enemy appears: **a copy of Raju himself,** from a distance, walking away, never letting him catch up. *(This is the man Meera waited for. He is not the man who came back.)*
- **Truth revealed here:** that the good life Raju remembers is a life he was *never actually in.*

### 4. CAVE — *"his subconscious"*

Accessed only after the player has exhausted both the night world and the day world. The fence beyond the night world opens; the scree slope descends; the cave mouth waits.

The cave is the deepest realm. It is not a dungeon. It is **a confession the player is forced to make on Raju's behalf.** Seven chambers, each a buried memory, each solvable only by standing inside it and *not running.* See the existing Act V breakdown for the full chamber-by-chamber design — it stays exactly as specified.

The cave cannot be re-entered. Once Raju has descended and climbed back out, the cave mouth is gone. The scree slope is smooth. The fence is just a fence. *You only get to tell this confession once.*

- **Mechanical role:** linear, irreversible, climactic. The final stretch before the boss.
- **Tone:** the dream-logic of things you have refused to look at for a long time.
- **Enemies:** none in the traditional sense. The chambers *are* the enemies.
- **Truth revealed here:** the child. The lost child. The letter in his son's handwriting. The sonogram. What the final chamber's door opens into.

### HOW THE REALMS TALK TO EACH OTHER

Several load-bearing cross-realm rules, for the team to wire consistently:

1. **The Stranger exists only in the night world, at the fence.** He cannot cross the portal into the day. He cannot enter the hall. He cannot enter the cave. His confinement is the first clue to his identity — he is waiting at the one threshold he was never allowed to cross: *being born.*
2. **Meera exists only in the cave, and faintly in the day world (as humming, as laundry, as absence).** She never appears in the night world. She never appears in the hall until the final crawl. Her absence from 90% of the game is the wound the player eventually names.
3. **The brass lamp appears once in every realm.** In the hall, on the side table. In the night world, in a neighbor's overturned kitchen. In the day world, on Meera's bedside, *lit.* In the cave, in the Thing's hand. One lamp. Four places. The player will notice by playthrough two.
4. **The yellow baby socks appear once in every realm.** On the sofa in the hall (Act I). On the Stranger's left foot in the night world. On the kitchen floor of the day world (final visit). On a paper star in the cave's nursery chamber. They are the game's recurring proof that the child was real.
5. **The three-note motif.** Meera hums it in the cave. The Stranger hums it in the night world when the player turns away. It plays faintly under the day world's ambient music. In the hall, it is the broken tune of the wall clock. Every realm knows the song.
6. **Blood only appears in the hall.** Until it doesn't. Its first spillover is into the day world, by Visit 6 — a small drop, ignorable. Then the night world, between expeditions, in the shape of Raju's own footprints walking somewhere he did not go. Then the cave, in the final chamber, under the door. The blood is the truth leaking through the seals between realms. By the end, every realm is bleeding, and only the hall remembers why.

### PACING ACROSS THE REALMS

Rough shape of a full playthrough, for the design team:

| Phase | Player time | Realms visited |
|---|---|---|
| Opening (Act I) | 15 min | Hall |
| First night run | 30–45 min | Hall → Night → Hall |
| First day visit | 20 min | Hall → Night → **Day** → Night → Hall |
| Night/Day cycling | 2–3 hours | loops, escalating corruption |
| Stranger's final blessing | 10 min | Night (fence opens) |
| The descent | 1.5–2 hours | Cave (linear, irreversible) |
| Boss + waking | 20 min | Hall (warped) → Hall (real) |

The rhythm is **hub → hunted → lied-to → confessed → waking.** The player cycles between the hunted realm (night) and the lying realm (day), always returning to the hub (hall) that knows the truth but isn't speaking yet, until finally the confession in the cave earns them the right to go back to the hall one last time — and this time, the hall tells them everything.

## THE STRANGER — A CHARACTER STUDY
### *(Bestiary entry. The most important "enemy" that is not an enemy.)*

He is the centerpiece of the game's horror. Every other creature in the bestiary is something that *hunts* Raju. The Stranger is something far more unbearable: **a thing that loves him.** Unconditional love from something whose face you cannot see, whose origin you cannot place, and whose presence you cannot refuse, is a species of terror that survival horror almost never uses. This game will.

Design him so that, on first meeting, the player feels *relieved.* On the second meeting, uneasy. On the third, they start avoiding the fence. And by the end, when they realize what he is, they will want to play the entire game again just to look at him differently.

### SILHOUETTE

- **Height.** He reads as tall. Six-and-a-half feet, maybe seven. But this is a lie the camera is telling. His actual body, under the robe, is **the size of an eight-year-old child.** The hood is propped up on nothing. The shoulders are the shape of nothing. The robe hangs from a frame of bent sticks and air. Camera tricks, forced perspective, and subtle vertex scaling should be used in-engine so the player never quite catches the discrepancy — but in low-light scenes, his silhouette briefly *shrinks* when the player blinks (i.e., when a subtle eye-blink camera post-effect triggers). They will feel it before they notice it.
- **Proportions.** Shoulders too narrow for the height. Arms that hang a few inches past where arms should stop. Neck that, when the hood shifts, appears too long by half a vertebra.
- **Posture.** He never slouches. He stands with the ramrod-straight patience of a child who has been told not to fidget at a wedding. The stillness is the first thing that will wrong-foot the player.

### THE ROBE

- **Color.** Grey. But not a flat grey — a grey that changes depending on what is behind him. Against the dead sky, he is the color of mourning. Against the house, he is the color of old cement. Against Meera (when she appears in Chamber 3 onward), he is **slightly yellow,** the exact shade of the baby socks on the sofa.
- **Material.** A heavy wool cassock, floor-length. The hem is frayed and **wet** — always wet — dragging a dark line behind him wherever he walks, even across dry stone. This trail dries and disappears within three seconds. The player will only catch it in peripheral vision.
- **Movement.** The robe moves *one beat behind* his body. When he turns his head, the fabric of the hood turns a half-second later, like it's reluctant to follow him.
- **The breathing.** At rest, the robe rises and falls slowly at the chest, as if he is breathing. He is not. The hood is empty. **Something else is breathing inside the robe.** Something smaller, lower, closer to the belly.

### THE HOOD

- Deep. Unnaturally deep. A cone of darkness that should not contain so much darkness, given the lighting.
- The first time the player looks into the hood, it is **empty.** Just dark fabric folding into itself.
- On the second look, **there is something white inside.** A shape. It is not a face. It might be the back of a skull. It might be a folded paper. It might be an ultrasound photograph.
- On the third look — and this should happen exactly once in the game, scripted, in the Chamber 5 hotel room — the hood is **full of eyes.** Small, dark, animal eyes, dozens of them, all looking at Raju with an expression the player will not be able to name but will recognize. It is the look a newborn gives its father when it has never seen him before but already knows his voice. It lasts less than a second. The hood goes dark again. He does not mention it.

### THE HANDS

**This is the detail that should haunt the player.**

The Stranger's sleeves are long. When he reaches out to hand Raju an item — a match, a canteen, a folded cloth — his hands emerge from the cuffs. The player will see them at a glance and move on.

They are not adult hands.

They are the size of **a child's hands, maybe six or seven years old.** Pink palms. Soft, unscarred skin. Fingernails bitten short. But the sleeves are cut to an adult's reach, so the hands emerge from a tunnel of fabric that is far too big for them — you can see all the way up the dark of the sleeve before the small hand appears at the end of it.

No NPC in the game ever comments on this. The game never lampshades it. It is simply *true,* and the player will either notice it in Act II or re-watch a cutscene after finishing the game and scream.

### THE FEET

Bare. Bare under the hem of the robe. Small, pink, soft, *clean* in a way that bare feet should never be in a dead grey world. On the left foot is a single **yellow baby sock,** half-pulled-on, sliding off the heel. Meera laid a pair of those socks on the sofa an hour before Raju came home. The player sees them in Act I. They will not make the connection until Act VII.

### THE FACE

There is no face. There has never been a face. The player must never see one until the ending.

But in certain frames, in certain lighting, when the Stranger tilts his head just so, *something flickers* in the darkness of the hood — a shape that is almost a child's face, almost Meera's cheekbones, almost Raju's mouth, stitched together from impressions and gone before the player can focus.

### THE VOICE

- **Warm.** Dangerously warm. The voice of someone who has been waiting for Raju in a well-lit kitchen for a very long time and is not angry that he is late, only glad that he is home.
- **Patient.** Never raised. Never hurried.
- **Knowing.** He uses Raju's childhood nickname ("*Raju-beta*") twice in the game — a name only Raju's late mother ever called him. Raju does not react. The player will.
- **Slightly wrong pitch.** Subtly layered: the main voice is a calm adult man, but underneath, quiet enough that the player will assume it's ambient noise, is a **child's voice saying the same words half a beat later.** Mix this so it registers as uncanny before the player consciously identifies it. Many players will hear it only on second playthrough with headphones.

### THE CADENCE OF SOMEONE WHO HAS LOVED YOU FOR A LONG TIME

Write every Stranger line with this rule: **he speaks like someone answering questions Raju forgot he asked years ago.** Not cryptic — *familiar.* He knows the rhythm of Raju's thinking. He finishes Raju's sentences one word early, not one word late. He never uses his name unless it matters. When he does use it, it's a blow.

Example lines, for tone:

> "You always take the long way. Even when we used to walk to the shop, you would take the long way. Do you remember?"
> *(Raju does not remember. The player will recognize that the Stranger is remembering something that never happened — or something Raju has forgotten was meant to.)*

> "I am not angry. I am never angry. I think you think I should be, and I am sorry that I cannot be, for you."

> "You don't have to explain. I already know the part you're afraid to say."

> "Go slowly. There's no one waiting who hasn't already waited."

### UNCANNY TELLS (for the team to seed throughout the game)

Not all at once. One or two per scene. The player should feel *haunted* without being able to list why.

1. **He casts two shadows.** A tall thin one under the robe, and a small compact one directly beneath him, roughly the size of a child. They do not always agree on the angle of the sun.
2. **He appears in reflections half a second before he appears in the scene.** When the player approaches a window or a puddle, his reflection is there first, already waiting. The real him walks into frame a beat later.
3. **He never crosses the fence.** Not once. Not ever. He can lean against it. He can rest his small hand on the latch. He cannot step through. This is a physical law of the world. The player will test it. He will smile.
4. **He stands exactly four feet from Raju.** Always. Not three. Not five. The distance Meera was lying from Raju on the hall floor.
5. **He hums Meera's three-note motif when the player turns the camera away from him.** It stops the moment the player turns back. Many players will spend an hour trying to catch him doing it. He will let them, eventually, just once.
6. **Birds will not land near him.** There are no birds in the game's dead world except one — a single black bird that appears twice, far away, on the horizon. It flies away whenever the Stranger turns toward it.
7. **Objects he has touched feel warm.** When Raju picks up a supply the Stranger handed him, the item's flavor text ends with a single extra sentence: *"It is warm, as if it has been held."*
8. **He never eats, drinks, or sits.** The player will notice the lack of these behaviors only in retrospect. He stands. He waits. He hands things over. He waits again.
9. **He weeps without sound.** Once, in Act IV, just before the gate opens onto the scree slope, if the player lingers on the Stranger's face for more than ten seconds, a single wet streak runs down the darkness inside the hood. He does not acknowledge it. He does not wipe it away.

### THE ONE TIME HE IS WRONG

In Act V, Chamber 4 (The Letters) — inside the cave — the player can walk to the far end of the library and find a single letter that is **not in Meera's handwriting.** It is in a child's handwriting. Pencil on ruled paper. Misspellings. It reads:

> *"dear papa. i know you coudnt see me. it is ok. i saw you. i think you are nice. i think mama is nice too. pleese be nice to her. i waited for a long time. i am not angry. i was never angry. love your son."*

The Stranger is not in this chamber. He cannot follow Raju into the cave. But when the player reads this letter, the audio track that has been silent for the whole chamber plays, faintly, far away: **the humming. The three-note motif. In a child's voice, unaccompanied.**

This is the only time the mask slips before the ending. The player will not know why they are crying.

### THE REVEAL (in Act VII — the waking, in the real hall)

When the Stranger lowers his hood, he is a small boy, eight years old, with Meera's eyes and Raju's mouth. He is dressed in a white shirt. He is wearing one yellow sock and one bare foot. He has been waiting, between his parents' bleeding bodies, for his father to finally *come outside* — not of the house, but of himself.

He takes his mother's hand. He takes his father's hand. He says:

> *"You took the long way. But you came."*

The player will hear, in that line, the thing that has been underneath his voice all game: **the child's voice and the man's voice speaking together, finally in tune.**

## THE TRUE TIMELINE (what actually happened, in order)

Write this down. Tape it to your monitor. *Do not let the player learn this in order.*

1. Three years ago, Raju and Meera lost their first pregnancy at five months. Meera went quiet for a long time. Raju went loud.
2. Eighteen months ago, Raju met Kaveri at a client dinner. He told himself it was nothing. It was nothing. Until it wasn't.
3. Six months ago, Meera found out. She did not confront him. She started writing letters to herself in a notebook she hid behind the rice tin.
4. Two months ago, Meera found out she was pregnant again. She did not tell Raju. She was not sure she wanted him to be the father of the child she already loved.
5. One week ago, Meera decided she would tell him tonight.
6. Tonight, Raju came home late. Kaveri's perfume was on his collar. Meera smelled it from the doorway and something inside her, something that had been holding up the ceiling of their marriage for three years, simply put itself down and walked out.
7. They argued in the hall.
8. She told him about the baby.
9. He said the wrong thing. Not a cruel thing. A *small* thing. He said: *"Are you sure it's mine?"*
10. She picked up the brass lamp from the side table.
11. He grabbed her wrist to stop her.
12. The lamp came down anyway — his hand, her hand, both — and struck her temple.
13. She fell. She was still breathing.
14. In the panic, he fell too, backwards, onto the glass of the shattered photograph they had knocked off the wall. A long shard went through his abdomen, just under the ribs. He did not feel it for almost a full minute.
15. They lay four feet apart on the floor of the hall, both bleeding, both conscious, both unable to reach the phone.
16. Raju's brain, rather than accept this, *built a world.*

The entire game takes place in the forty-seven seconds between Raju hitting the floor and Raju dying. Meera is dying beside him the whole time. She is trying to say something to him. He cannot hear her over the sound of his own story.

## ACT I — THE HOUSE THAT REMEMBERS

### Setting: The Hall Interior. *(Realm: **Hall Interior.**)*

The tutorial. The argument. The fall.

The player begins *inside* the fight. Mid-sentence. Meera is already crying and Raju does not know why — because Raju has never actually been listening. The player controls him through a slow walk from the doorway to the center of the hall while Meera's voice layers over itself, saying things that Raju is not hearing:

> *"I waited. I waited so long for you to come home as the person I married."*
> *"Do you know how many nights I have rehearsed this?"*
> *"I am not angry. I am finished. There is a difference."*

Environmental storytelling to plant without explanation (the player will not notice any of it on the first pass; every item returns later with new meaning):

- A wedding photograph on the wall, slightly crooked.
- A brass lamp on the side table. Heavy. Ornamental. A gift from her mother.
- A notebook tucked behind the rice tin in the kitchen, barely visible from the hall.
- A small wooden crib disassembled and stacked in the corner of the hall, half-covered by a sheet. Raju has been meaning to take it to the storage room for three years.
- A doctor's appointment card on the refrigerator. Dated tomorrow. The player cannot read the name on it yet.
- A single pair of baby socks, yellow, folded on the arm of the sofa. Meera put them there an hour before Raju came home. She was going to show him.

**The fight beat-by-beat:**

Raju speaks. The player chooses nothing — his lines are scripted, because *Raju does not control what Raju says*. This is the first and most important rule of the game. The player is not piloting Raju. The player is *remembering* Raju, from the inside, helpless.

Meera picks up the lamp.
Raju grabs her wrist.
The camera cuts.
The screen goes white, not black. White like fluorescent hospital light. Like the ceiling you stare at when you cannot move your head.

When it returns, Raju is alone on the floor. Meera is not there. The lamp is not there. The blood is not there.

The crib in the corner is now fully assembled.

No one assembled it.

## ACT II — THE NIGHT WORLD

### Setting: The yard. The fence. The changed world. *(Realm: **Night World.**)*

Raju stands up. He feels *fine*. (He is not fine. A shard of glass is inside him. His brain has simply decided not to tell him yet.)

He opens the front door expecting the street. He gets something else.

The world outside is his neighborhood, but **drained of depth** — flat colors, long shadows, a sky the color of an old bruise that never sets. The sun does not move. His neighbors' houses are there, but the windows are painted on. The trees do not have undersides to their leaves. It is a world built by someone who only saw his neighborhood from the inside of a moving car, once, a long time ago.

The fence around his yard is **taller than he remembers.** He cannot recall it ever being this tall. He cannot recall there ever being a fence at all.

Beyond the fence, the world **hums**. A low sound, not quite mechanical, not quite biological. The player's controller should vibrate faintly whenever they face the fence.

### THE STRANGER

He is waiting by the gate. He has been waiting a long time. The player should feel this in the way he stands — the slight bend of patience, like a father who has been waiting on a porch for a son who said he'd be home by eight.

> **STRANGER:** "You took the long way."
> **RAJU:** "Who are you?"
> **STRANGER:** "Someone who has been waiting."
> **RAJU:** "For what?"
> **STRANGER:** "For you to come outside."

He does not give Raju his name. He tells Raju three things:

1. Meera is not here. She is *out there*, somewhere beyond the fence. She is waiting to be found.
2. Beyond the fence, death is real in a way it is not here. Raju must gather supplies before he crosses — food, water, light, warmth, a weapon.
3. He must not, under any circumstances, turn and look at the house while he is gathering supplies. *"The house will try to talk to you. It has a lot to say. Do not listen until you are ready. You are not ready."*

The player, of course, will turn and look at the house. This is a game. This is what players do. And every time they do, the house **flickers** — for a single frame, the windows are red from the inside, the door is open, Meera is standing in the doorway with her mouth moving. She is saying something. The player cannot hear it yet.

### SUPPLIES ARE NOT SUPPLIES

This is the central mechanic lie of the game, and it is the most important one:

Every "supply" Raju collects in the world is, in reality, **a thing from his marriage that he ignored.**

- **"Canned food"** from a neighbor's overturned shelf — is, on the item icon when examined closely, *a tiffin box Meera used to pack him for work. He stopped opening them six months ago. They piled up in the car. He threw them out without telling her.*
- **"Clean water"** — is a glass of water she left by his bedside every night for a year and he never drank.
- **"Rope"** — is the sari she wore the day they got married. He does not recognize it.
- **"A flashlight"** — is the small lamp from her side of the bed. The one she used to read by after he fell asleep.
- **"Bandages"** — are strips torn from the bedsheets of the room they were supposed to make into a nursery. The room they never talked about again.
- **"A knife"** — is the kitchen knife she used to cut vegetables every morning. He will need it later. He will use it for something it was never meant for.

The player does not know this on the first pickup. They will notice, gradually, that the item descriptions are wrong. That the flavor text is written in **first person, past tense, from Meera's perspective.**

> **"I left this by your side of the bed every night. I thought, one day, when he is thirsty in the dark, he will remember me."**

This is when the game begins to turn.

### THE NPCS AT THE EDGE

As Raju explores the yard and the street beyond it (still within the fence's radius), he meets **distorted versions of people he has wronged or ignored.** None of them have faces. All of them know him.

- **The Mailman** — hands him a stack of letters, all addressed to Meera in her own handwriting. "She wrote to herself for a year. She asked me not to tell you."
- **The Neighbor Boy** — six years old, plays with a toy car in the driveway. "Auntie Meera used to give me sweets. Where did she go? I thought she lived here."
- **Kaveri** — appears *once*, at the very end of the street, standing under a streetlight that is the only light in the world. She does not speak. She does not have a face. She has *Meera's wedding bangles on her wrist*. When Raju approaches, she walks away, and the streetlight goes out.

These NPCs are not enemies. They are also not friends. They are testimony.

### THE BESTIARY (what the monsters actually are)

As Raju moves further into the night world, the bestiary awakens. None of the creatures are random spawns. Each is a piece of him, wearing fur and teeth:

- **Hellhound — his appetite.** Appears first in the overturned street near Kaveri's perfume bottle on the curb.
- **Shadow Spider — his lies.** Nests in the corners of the cleanest rooms; Meera always hated spiders.
- **Banshee Ghost — Meera's unspoken grief.** Floats through walls. Flees flashlight. Her scream is, if the player slows the audio, *a sentence:* "*Why did you stop listening.*"
- **Shadow Wraith — his own death.** The glass shard inside him, personified. Cannot be killed, only held at bay by light. Every time Raju lowers the flashlight to rest his arm, the wraith is a step closer.
- **Skull Specter — the unborn child's anger.** The only creature that sometimes just… stops. Sits in the dirt. Burns quietly. Won't attack until approached.

**Combat is not a victory system. It is a dialogue system.** Each enemy killed drops a single item whose flavor text is a line from the marriage, assigned to that enemy's archetype. The player assembles the truth by killing the things that have been trying to tell it to him.

### THE PORTAL

Somewhere in the middle distance of the night world, Raju finds **a tear in the air** — a rectangle of warm yellow light, suspended a foot above the ground, with sun inside it. The sun moves.

The Stranger, from his place at the fence, raises a small hand.

> **STRANGER:** "You can go through. I cannot. It is not for me. It is for you to remember what you had."

The portal leads to the day world. See Act III.

The player can cycle between night and day as many times as they like. The game will not stop them. It will only *change* them.

---

## ACT III — THE DAY WORLD

### Setting: The neighborhood, as he wants to remember it. *(Realm: **Day World,** accessed via the portal inside the night world.)*

Raju steps through the rectangle of warm light and lands in his own street, but **alive.** Birds. Sun. The smell of cut grass. Meera's laundry on the line, white and moving slightly. His front door is open. Her slippers are in the entryway.

She is not home. She has gone to the market. There is a note on the counter in her handwriting:

> *"back soon. there is tea in the pot. I love you."*

The tea is still steaming.

The player, for the first time in the game, feels *safe.* This is the point. This is the trap.

### WHAT THE DAY WORLD GIVES

Not objects. **Memories.** Flavor-text items with no inventory weight, no use outside the cave, and no visual model. Each one is a single sentence, and the sentence is a small knife.

Examples of memory-supplies to collect:
- *"the way she laughed when you burned the rice — four years ago, you haven't made her laugh like that since."*
- *"the silence after you said nothing on her birthday last year."*
- *"the way she used to save the last piece of mango for you, even when you came home late."*
- *"the evening she asked if you were happy. you said 'of course' without looking up from your phone."*
- *"the night of the first loss. she held your hand. you did not hold hers back."*

These are required keys for the cave's chambers. Raju cannot descend without having stood, repeatedly, in the sunlight of the life he was too distracted to have.

### CORRUPTION, VISIT BY VISIT

The day world is not a safe room. It is a slow poison. See the **FOUR REALMS** document above for the exact corruption rules per visit — photographs losing Raju's face, the neighbor who never stops waving, Meera's humming always in the next room, the crack in the sky, the blood drop on the floor. Each visit, the player should feel less like they are coming home and more like they are *trespassing in a photograph.*

By the final visit, the day world is empty, the tea is cold, and there is a yellow baby sock on the kitchen floor. When Raju picks it up, the portal closes behind him. He cannot return.

### THE OTHER RAJU

At some corruption threshold (recommended: Visit 4), the player, walking down the street, sees **another Raju** at the far end, walking away. Same clothes. Same gait. He is going somewhere. He does not turn around when Raju calls.

The other Raju always stays at the same distance. No matter how fast the player runs, he cannot close the gap.

This is the man Meera was waiting for. It is not the man who came home.

The day world's gentlest horror is that Raju will never catch himself.

---

## ACT IV — THE FENCE, AND WHAT WAITS JUST PAST IT

Raju gathers enough supplies. He returns to the fence.

The Stranger is still there. He has not moved.

> **STRANGER:** "Are you ready?"
> **RAJU:** "Yes."
> **STRANGER:** "You are not. But I will not stop you. I have never been able to stop you."

He opens the gate.

Beyond the fence, the world **falls away.** Not metaphorically — literally. The road ends in a cliff. There is no horizon. There is a long slope of grey scree leading down into a valley, and in the valley there is a **cave mouth**, and the cave mouth is **shaped like a woman's open mouth, mid-sentence.**

This is the image the game should burn into the player: *a sentence no one finished.*

The walk down to the cave should be long. Deliberately long. Silent. The humming stops. The player's footsteps are the only sound. The Stranger does not follow.

When Raju reaches the cave mouth, there is a body lying across the threshold. Not Meera. Not anyone the player knows.

It is **Raju himself.** Younger. Maybe twenty-three. Wearing the shirt he wore the day he proposed. The body is not dead. It is sleeping. When Raju steps over it, it whispers, without opening its eyes:

> *"You used to be someone else."*

Raju enters the cave.

---

## ACT V — THE CAVE OF ECHOES

*(Realm: **Cave.** Accessed only after both the night world's fence has opened and the day world has corrupted to its endpoint. The cave is linear and one-way — once descended and climbed back out, it is gone.)*


### Structure

The cave is **seven chambers deep.** Each chamber is a memory Raju has buried. Each chamber must be solved, not fought. The cave is not a dungeon. It is a confession the player is forced to make on Raju's behalf.

The player should not initially understand this. The chambers look like survival-horror set pieces. They are, mechanically. But everything inside them is **salvaged from the marriage.**

#### Chamber 1 — THE KITCHEN THAT WOULD NOT END

A kitchen, looping. Every time the player walks through the doorway, they come back into the same kitchen. Meera is there, with her back turned, cooking. She hums a song. Raju cannot get her to turn around. On the counter, food piles up: biryani, kheer, the dishes she used to make for him before he started coming home late. Each loop, the food gets colder. Each loop, her humming gets quieter.

To escape: the player must **stand still for ninety seconds** and listen. Not interact. Not walk. Not touch anything. Just listen to her humming until it ends.

On the floor behind her, when she finally stops, there is a plate she had set for him, covered with a cloth. Under the cloth: the plate is empty. It has been empty for a long time.

The doorway opens.

#### Chamber 2 — THE ROOM THAT WAS GOING TO BE A NURSERY

A small room with a single crib in the center. The crib is empty. The walls are painted half-blue, half-unpainted — she started, he never finished. A mobile of paper stars hangs over the crib. When Raju approaches, the stars begin to fall, one by one, and each one is a **paper folded around a date.** The dates are the twelve weeks of the first pregnancy. And then five more dates — the weeks of the *second* pregnancy, the one Raju does not yet know about.

The twelfth and final star, when it falls, unfolds into a **sonogram photo.** The player can pick it up. It becomes a key item. The player will not understand why.

The crib, when the player looks inside it a second time, is no longer empty. There is a small wet shape under a blanket. The player cannot lift the blanket. The game will not let them. *Not yet.*

#### Chamber 3 — THE MIRROR HALL

A corridor of mirrors. Raju walks forward. In every mirror, his reflection is *slightly behind him.* Slightly slower. Slightly sadder. In the final mirror, the reflection is **not Raju.** It is Meera. She is mouthing something. The player must lip-read it to proceed. The words are:

> *"I tried to tell you."*

When the player speaks those words (by pressing an input prompt), the mirror shatters, and the reflection steps through, and Meera — *this version of her, this cave-ghost version* — walks past him without looking at him, toward the next chamber.

She will lead him now. He will follow her for the rest of the cave.

#### Chamber 4 — THE LETTERS

A library. Endless shelves. Every book is her handwriting. Every book is the notebook from behind the rice tin, multiplied, the same text over and over in different handwritings of different ages of her life. The player can pick up any book and read a page. The pages are the letters she wrote to herself for six months after she discovered the affair. A selection to rotate:

> *"I am writing this because I have no one else to tell. The woman I was before I met him would have left. I don't know where that woman went."*

> *"He came home tonight and kissed my forehead and I almost told him. I almost said it. I almost said, I know. But he looked so tired. I didn't want him to be tired."*

> *"I am pregnant again. I have not told him. I don't know if I want him to be the father of this one. I don't know if he wants to be. I don't know if I have the right to choose for him. I don't know if he has the right to choose."*

> *"If I tell him tomorrow and he says the wrong thing, I will know. I will finally, finally know."*

> *"I hope he says the right thing."*

The player can read as many or as few as they want. The game tracks it. The ending changes subtly — not in what happens, but in what Meera says at the end — based on how many letters the player reads. A player who reads none will get an ending that feels cruel. A player who reads all of them will get an ending that feels like a confession. The difference is the same. It is still the end. It is just that one player will *understand* and the other will not.

#### Chamber 5 — THE OTHER WOMAN

A hotel room. Sterile. White sheets. Kaveri is on the bed, facedown, unmoving. The player is forced to approach. When Raju turns her over, her face is **Meera's.** Then it is Kaveri's. Then it is Meera's. It flickers between them like a loose bulb. She does not speak.

On the bedside table: a framed photograph of Raju and Meera on their wedding day. Raju's face has been scratched out.

To leave the room, Raju must pick up the photograph. When he does, **the scratching is on his face too, now, in the reflection of the window.**

He cannot un-scratch it. He carries it for the rest of the game.

#### Chamber 6 — THE CHILD

A single empty room. A crib. A soft light. A small sound — the sound of a heartbeat, too fast, the way a fetal heartbeat sounds on an ultrasound speaker.

The sonogram photo from Chamber 2 **begins to glow in the inventory.** The player must place it in the crib.

When they do, the heartbeat slows. And slows. And stops.

The room goes dark.

When the light returns, the crib is gone. The wooden pieces of the crib from the hall of Raju's house are stacked neatly against the wall, as they were at the start of the game, covered by a sheet.

Raju falls to his knees. The game takes control away from the player for the first time. Raju weeps. It is not pretty. It is not scored with music. It is a small, ugly, choking sound, and it goes on for longer than the player will be comfortable with.

When he stands, he is bleeding from the abdomen. He did not notice before. The cave is no longer hiding it from him.

#### Chamber 7 — THE DOOR AT THE BOTTOM

A single door. No room. Just the door, standing in the dark, with light leaking from underneath.

Meera — the cave-ghost — is waiting beside it.

> **MEERA:** "The last thing is on the other side."
> **RAJU:** "Is it you?"
> **MEERA:** "It is what you think I am."

She opens the door. She does not go through.

## ACT VI — THE BELLY OF THE BEAST

*(Realm: **Hall Interior,** warped — the boss arena is the hall with its proportions gone wrong.)*


### The Boss

The final chamber is **the hall of Raju's house.**

But it is wrong. The ceiling is too high. The walls breathe. The wedding photograph is enormous, covering an entire wall, and Raju's face has been scratched out of it by something with very long fingernails.

In the center of the hall stands **THE THING.**

It is the shape of a woman. It is wearing Meera's red wedding sari. Its hair is long and wet. It is taller than it should be. Its arms are too long. It is holding the brass lamp from the side table.

It does not have a face.
Where a face should be, there is a **mouth, vertical, running from forehead to chin, full of small white stones that might be teeth or might be the paper stars from the nursery.**

It speaks in Meera's voice. It says:

> *"You should not have said that."*

The fight begins.

### The mechanics of the fight

This fight is designed to feel *unfair* and *slow* and *wrong.* The player should want it to end. They should be tired by this point. That is intentional.

- The Thing moves deliberately. It is not fast. It is **inevitable.**
- It strikes with the lamp. One hit takes most of the player's health.
- The kitchen knife (collected in Act II) is the only weapon that damages it.
- Every time the player damages it, **it speaks.** Not in Meera's voice now. In *Raju's.*

> *(stab)* "I didn't mean it."
> *(stab)* "I was tired."
> *(stab)* "She didn't mean anything."
> *(stab)* "I was going to tell you."
> *(stab)* "I was going to be better."
> *(stab)* "I was going to come home."

- At half health, The Thing stops attacking. It stands still. It opens its arms. It is inviting him in.

The player can wait. If they wait for ninety seconds without attacking, a secret scene plays — Meera's cave-ghost appears beside Raju, takes his hand, and the fight ends. There is no boss kill. There is only an embrace. This ending exists. It is the **"true" ending.** Almost no player will find it.

Most players will keep stabbing.

- At low health, The Thing kneels. It does not defend itself.
- The final strike must be manually aimed. A prompt appears: **AIM.**
- The player can aim at the head, the chest, or the belly.
- Most players will aim at the belly. It is the largest target. The game has been teaching them, all along, to aim at the belly. The knife has a prompt that says *"thrust low."* The Stranger, back at the fence, said *"gut the thing that waits for you."* The cave walls, scratched in a dozen places, said *"in the belly."*
- **The Thing is cradling something in its belly.**
- When the player thrusts, the knife goes in easy. Too easy.

The Thing falls.
The sari falls open.
There is a **small shape** curled inside it.

Not a fetus. Not a monster. A paper folded around a date: *week twelve.*

The player has stabbed the child Meera was carrying.

The player has stabbed it themselves, with their own hand, while aiming carefully, in a fight they chose to keep fighting instead of laying down the knife.

This is not a jumpscare. This is not a twist. This is the truth the game has been trying to tell Raju, and the player, for the entire runtime. Raju did not lose his wife. Raju *ended* something, and he has been building a world to pretend he didn't.

The Thing looks up at him. Its face is forming now. Slowly. Meera's features. Meera's eyes. Meera's mouth.

She is smiling. Not cruelly. **Sadly.** Like someone who always knew.

> **MEERA:** "There you are."

---

## ACT VII — THE WAKING

*(Realm: **Hall Interior,** real — the warping ends, the ceiling comes down to the right height, the wedding photograph is only slightly crooked. The hub becomes, at last, what it always was: a crime scene.)*


### The hall, again.

The camera pulls back. The boss arena dissolves. The ceiling is normal. The walls are normal. The wedding photograph is on the wall, only slightly crooked.

Raju is on the floor of the hall of his own house.
Meera is on the floor four feet away from him.
They are both bleeding.

He can crawl. The game gives the player control one last time. The only input that works is **move.** They cannot run. They cannot stand. They cannot speak. They cannot pick anything up. They can only crawl toward her.

It takes a long time.

When he reaches her, she is still conscious. Barely. She looks at him. He looks at her.

She says her final line. The line you gave me to work with. But expanded, because she has one breath left and she wants to spend it:

> **MEERA:** "I have loved you so much.
> But you…
> …you were never actually here, were you?
> Not in the hall.
> Not at the table.
> Not in the bed.
> Not in the room we were going to paint.
> I waited.
> I waited for you to come home as the man I married.
> I think he died a long time ago.
> I think I have been married to his ghost.
> …
> Tell the baby…
> Tell the baby I tried."

She dies.

Raju cannot tell the baby anything. The baby is inside her, and the baby is already gone. He has known this, in a way, since Chamber 6.

He bleeds out beside her. His hand is on her hand. He is trying to apologize. He runs out of breath before he finds the word.

The screen goes white. Not black. White.

---

## THE FINAL REVEAL

As the white fades, **the Stranger is standing in the hall.**

He walks to the bodies. He kneels between them. He lowers his hood.

The Stranger is **a child. Maybe eight years old.** A boy. He has Meera's eyes and Raju's mouth. He is wearing a small white shirt. He is the child that was never born. He has been waiting, in the space between his parents, for his entire non-life, for his father to come outside.

He takes his mother's hand in one hand.
He takes his father's hand in the other.
He closes his eyes.

> **THE CHILD:** "You took the long way.
> But you came."

The screen goes black.

Credits.

## THE NEW GAME + LAYER (optional, for the truly broken)

If the player starts a new game, something is different.

The hall is the same. The argument is the same. But now, in the opening scene, **when Meera picks up the lamp, the player can choose not to grab her wrist.**

If they don't, she does not swing it. She lowers it. She sits on the floor. She puts her head in her hands. She says:

> *"Please. Please just sit down. Please just let me tell you."*

The player can sit down beside her. If they do, she tells him. About the baby. About the letters. About the fact that she already knows about Kaveri. About the fact that she is not leaving, if he is willing to be here. Actually here.

The game **ends in the hall.** There is no outside world. There is no fence. There is no stranger. There is no cave. There is no boss. There is no awakening.

Just a man sitting on the floor of his hall with his wife, listening, for the first time, for the rest of his life.

Credits roll over the two of them, sitting there, as the sun comes up through the window.

**This ending requires having played the full nightmare at least once.** You cannot skip to the good ending. You have to *know what it costs.* You have to have stabbed the thing in the belly and seen what was inside. Then, and only then, are you allowed to walk through the door a different way.

The game will not tell the player this ending exists. They will have to discover it by refusing the violence that the first playthrough rewarded.

## MOTIFS TO REPEAT RELENTLESSLY

These should recur in level art, SFX, UI, dialogue — anywhere the player might absorb them subconsciously.

- **THE BRASS LAMP.** It appears in the hall. In the cave. In the kitchen chamber, on the counter. In the cave-ghost's hand. In the boss's hand. It is the same lamp, always. Its weight is the weight of the story.
- **WEEK TWELVE.** Written on walls. Written on receipts. On calendars. On graffiti outside the fence. The player will not know what it means until Chamber 2. They will see it a dozen times before then and dismiss it.
- **SOCKS, YELLOW, SMALL.** On the sofa in Act I. In the cave-ghost's pocket in Chamber 3. In the boss's hair, tangled, in the final fight. In the child's hand at the end.
- **"ARE YOU SURE IT'S MINE."** The exact line Raju said that broke the marriage. It appears nowhere in-game until the very end, when the player hears it in Raju's own voice during the boss fight, on the final stab. The player will recognize it, even if they can't place why.
- **CROOKED WEDDING PHOTOGRAPH.** In every interior in the game, somewhere, there is a wedding photograph on a wall, slightly crooked. The player will straighten dozens of them across a playthrough. None of them stay straight.
- **HER HUMMING.** A three-note motif. Meera hums it in Chamber 1. Kaveri hums it, faintly, in Chamber 5 (to imply that Raju taught Kaveri the song Meera used to hum, without realizing). The child hums it in the final scene. The main menu music, when the player returns to it after finishing the game for the first time, is this three-note motif, and only this, played very slowly on a single string.

## DESIGN NOTES FOR THE TEAM

- **The player must never feel powerful.** The combat must be heavy, awkward, slow. Victories must feel like exhalations, not triumphs.
- **The supply mechanic must feel, at first, like generic survival busywork.** It is critical that the player not realize what the supplies *are* until they've been hoarding them for an hour. The reveal that every item was a piece of his marriage should land like a body blow.
- **The Stranger's voice must be warm.** He must never feel threatening. The player must like him. The gut-punch of the final reveal only works if the player feels they have lost a friend, not escaped a villain.
- **Meera must be more present in her absence than any other character is in their presence.** Her humming. Her handwriting. Her bangles on another woman's wrist. The weight of her should grow through the game until the player is begging to see her — and then the game gives her to them, cave-ghost Meera, Chamber 3 onward, and she does not speak unless she has to, and every word she does say should feel earned.
- **The child is not revealed as the Stranger until the very last shot.** Earlier hints are permitted but must be readable only on second playthrough. Examples: the Stranger's height (he stands shorter in Act II scenes than the camera framing suggests, an optical lie); his cadence ("someone who has been waiting" — waiting to be born); the fact that he is the only NPC in the outside world who has any warmth in his voice at all.
- **Raju does not speak in Acts II through V.** Only in the argument and in the final crawl. The player will get used to his silence. When he speaks in the end, trying to apologize, the sound of his voice will feel like a wound reopening.

---

## WHAT THIS STORY IS ACTUALLY ABOUT

It is about the specific cowardice of small men who are not cruel but are *absent.*
It is about the women who hold up the ceilings of their homes alone, for years, humming.
It is about the children who never get to be children because they were never given the chance to exist.
It is about the fact that you can love someone completely and still lose them, because love is not enough — **attention** is. And attention is the one thing Raju never learned to give.

The player, by the end, should not hate Raju. They should hate how much of Raju they recognize in themselves. That is the whole point. That is the "psychological kick." Not a monster under the bed. A monster in the mirror, wearing your own tired face, holding a lamp, saying a small thing at exactly the wrong time.

The game should end and the player should sit in the dark for a minute.

Then they should call someone they love and stay on the line a little longer than usual.

That is the win condition.
